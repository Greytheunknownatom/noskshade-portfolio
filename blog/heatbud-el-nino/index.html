I kept forgetting to drink water in the heat, so I designed and built HeatBud

With El Niño in the news and my days packed with work, I kept noticing the heat only after it had already got to me. So I made a small app to warn me first, and it’s live today.

Philip Nosike · 11 November 2026 · 8 min read

For weeks, everyone around me was talking about El Niño.

Hotter months. Drier air. Afternoons that feel heavier than the number on the weather app.

And I was too busy to notice any of it.

My days are mostly back-to-back design work. I’d look up mid-afternoon with a dull headache, realise my bottle was still full, and only then notice how hot the room had become.

That’s when it clicked: I wasn’t short of weather information. I was short of a nudge at the right time.

The problem with weather apps

Every weather app I opened gave me a number. 34°. 36°. Partly cloudy.

But a number doesn’t tell you what to do with it. Is it safe to go out at 1 PM? Should I move that errand to the evening? How much water do I actually need today?

A weather number is data. What people need in the heat is a decision.

And I’m one of the lucky ones who works indoors. Millions of Nigerians spend their whole day outside: riders, traders, builders, delivery staff, students walking to class. For them, heat isn’t an inconvenience. It decides how the day goes.

So I designed a buddy, not a dashboard

I gave myself a few rules before drawing a single screen:

  • Lead with how hot it feels, not the air temperature, because humidity changes everything.
  • Always say what to do next, in one plain sentence.
  • Make it feel like it was made here: Abuja on screen, Nigerian time, everyday English.
  • Keep it friendly. Heat is serious, but fear doesn’t make people open an app twice.

Open HeatBud and the first thing you see is the feels-like temperature over an illustrated Abuja scene, with a rating from Safe to Dangerous and a line like “OK with precautions until about 11 AM. Carry water, wear a hat, keep to shade.”

Underneath sits a heat dial, the best time to go out today, and a water bottle that fills up as you drink.

The water bottle took three tries

My first idea filled the whole Water page like a glass. It looked fun in a mockup. In use, it took over the screen and buried everything else.

So I moved water tracking onto Home as a real bottle with a side scale, the way you’d check one on your desk. The Water tab now holds the plan: a schedule across your day, with more water during the hottest hours, marked “do not skip.”

Your target comes from your weight, how active you are and the day’s forecast. Someone doing manual work outside gets a very different plan from someone at a desk.

The part I didn’t expect: safety

“Drink more water” sounds like harmless advice. It isn’t for everyone. People with heart, kidney or liver conditions, or on water pills, can be harmed by drinking too much.

So HeatBud asks. If a doctor has set a fluid limit, the app keeps your target inside it, and there’s a short guide to the warning signs of heat illness.

Designing for health means designing for the person the feature could hurt, not just the person it helps.

Becoming a designer who ships

HeatBud is also my first serious build. I’m learning to code by making things I designed myself, and this one taught me fast.

Push notifications were the hardest part. On the web they need a server and the right permissions on every phone. Dragging my files onto Netlify kept breaking that, so I learned to deploy from the command line instead. I also added calendar alarms as a backup, so reminders still fire with the app closed and no server involved.

And yes, there are twelve buddies. Sunny, Spike the cactus, Melon, Gecko and friends. Small things like that are why people come back.

Where it is now

HeatBud is live. It opens on any phone and installs to your home screen like an app. It has five tabs: Home, Forecast, Water, Alerts and Me.

It isn’t finished, and I don’t want it to be. Next I’m working on more reliable push alerts, more realistic landmark scenes, and, most of all, feedback from people who actually spend their day in the sun.

Try HeatBud on your phone and tell me what would make it more useful for you.
